A research report and concept for learners in low-income rural South Africa who depend on shared smartphones, prepaid data and an unreliable signal. Part 2 · Accessibility refinement

Designing LearnMate for learners with disabilities

My first concept tackled data, devices and signal, but it hadn't been designed with learners with disabilities in mind. Guided by the social model of disability, which says people are disabled by barriers in design rather than by their impairments, I audited my own prototype, mapped who it left out and rebuilt it around accessibility.

1. Auditing my own prototype

I checked all five wireframes against WCAG 2.2 Level AA, the seven principles of Universal Design and South Africa's National Strategic Framework on Universal Design and Access. I measured the contrast of every label and icon and looked for sensory, cognitive, physical and situational barriers.

WCAG asks for at least 4.5:1 for text and 3:1 for icons. The audit also found no read-aloud or text-size control, typed-only answers, a hidden microphone and only one home-language option.

2. Two new personas

Lwazi, 15

Grade 9 · Rural KwaZulu-Natal · isiZulu · Low vision

Has albinism and reads big, dark text with the phone close to his face. Borrows his grandmother's basic phone and wants to revise Natural Sciences without asking anyone to read to him.

Palesa, 17

Grade 11 · Rural Free State · seSotho · Dyslexia

Understands ideas quickly when they're explained aloud, but reads English slowly. Shares one phone with her family and gets about 20 minutes to study Maths Literacy.

Their journey maps showed the cascading effect of each barrier: Lwazi needing help on the very first screen, and the AI placing Palesa too low because it measured her reading speed, not her understanding.

3. The refined prototype

An AI-adaptive accessibility profile sits at the centre: what a learner picks on the set-up screen switches features on across the whole app, and the main features still work offline.

5. Researching the technology

I researched established and emerging assistive technologies, then weighed each against South African realities: affordability, connectivity, language diversity, long-term sustainability and cultural acceptance.

Considered but left out: VR headsets (too costly and data-heavy), AI sign-language avatars (not accurate enough, and South African Sign Language is best delivered by Deaf signers) and eye-tracking (needs extra hardware).

“Technology should adapt to the realities of its users, rather than expecting users to adapt to the limitations of technology.”

Both versions are built on secondary research and haven't yet been tested with learners. Next steps: co-design and test with learners with disabilities, their teachers and organisations such as the South African National Council for the Blind and the Deaf Federation of South Africa, and partner for affordable data and devices.
